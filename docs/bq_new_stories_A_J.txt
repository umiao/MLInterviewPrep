**Source:** PhD intern, consecutive summer internships

**Situation:** PhD intern had many long-open backlog tasks; peers gave feedback that his tickets appeared to be all self-learning with no clear deadlines. Upon investigation, found he actually had solid progress but lacked the skill to communicate it effectively.

**Result:** Intern improved his goal updates significantly, showcased contributions effectively, and received a return offer. Also discussed academia vs. industry differences (depth-first vs. stakeholder-aligned, XFN-heavy work style).

**Result:** Won across multiple relevance metrics + GMB win + increased user engagement. Project became production infrastructure adopted by ads and other teams to measure impact of their changes on user experience and relevance.

### STORY K: Ethical Dilemma — Seller Risk Modeling Fairness
**Source:** Search ranking MLE, risk guardrail review

**Situation:** As search ranking MLE, while reviewing risk guardrails, discovered that the sell risk model was systematically unfair to new sellers — they received high risk scores immediately after registration with zero transactions, creating a vicious cycle where new sellers could never build a reputation.

**Task:** Believed the approach needed to shift from seller-only modeling to seller-listing cross-modeling, allowing new sellers to be fairly evaluated through listing quality. However, this direction faced strong opposition from the principal researcher who designed the original system.

**Action:**
Phase 1 — Initial conflict at design review. Researcher's position: buyer complaints rising means we're trading revenue for buyer experience, must raise the seller bar. Understood his concern but believed this framing ignored a critical problem.

Phase 2 — Deep research after the meeting. Used PayPal and other industry cases to demonstrate that the tradeoff itself is part of compliance — how much false-positive transaction blocking are you willing to accept to prevent one real fraud? Pursuing absolute avoidance essentially requires whitelist mechanisms, making business impossible. Researched platform liability legal framework; found platform's core obligations focus on reporting mechanisms, info collection obligations, takedown procedures, and privacy protections. No evidence supported blanket unfriendly mechanisms toward new sellers — which could actually constitute a bigger legal risk.

Phase 3 — Escalation. Brought research to revision design phase to convince researcher, but failed. Judged the disagreement had exceeded pure technical scope. After aligning with manager, escalated to senior director and higher leadership.

**Result:** Leadership referenced the research and researcher's concerns, collaborated with legal department, and ultimately confirmed the precision modeling direction. This direction also aligned with the company's strategic push into recommerce.

**Defense Q&A:**
- "How do you view researcher's opposition?" — His concern was legitimate; buyer experience IS core. Disagreement wasn't about whether to protect buyers, but HOW. Blanket new-seller penalties are a lazy solution; precision modeling truly protects buyers without harming the ecosystem.
- "Was your escalation timing right?" — Did thorough research first, attempted working-level persuasion, only escalated after exhausting personal effort.
- "What if leadership chose researcher's approach?" — Escalation goal wasn't "let me win" but ensuring the decision was made with full information. Would disagree and commit, but ensured an informed decision process.

**BQ Questions Covered:**
1. Ethical dilemma in your work
2. Privacy/security concerns approach
3. Advocating for responsible practices
4. Persuading others to change direction
5. Difficult technical decision with non-obvious consequences

### STORY L: Tech Debt Balance — Declarative Artifactory Proof of Concept
**Source:** Search ranking MLE, feature delivery vs infrastructure dependency

**Situation:** Developing query triage and regex-based model features that should deploy on the team's new declarative artifactory system. But the system was repeatedly delayed, blocking feature delivery.

**Task:** Find a path that unblocks feature delivery without creating a throwaway. Core question: which values of the declarative system can be captured now?

**Action:**
Phase 1 — Technical insight. Deep research revealed the declarative system's essence: you can hand-write the JSON ranking rule expr and string that would otherwise require Scala/C++ repo builds. The real blockers were storage, versioning, and deployment infrastructure — not generating the expr itself.

Phase 2 — Solution design & validation. Designed approach using eBay's internal caching system for storage, injecting named expr as params into the search engine. Technical challenge: generating reliable code outside the large search system context and safely injecting it. Built parity tests and tooling proving hand-generated named expr was identical to profiling-layer generated ones.

Phase 3 — Driving adoption. Demoed equivalence to team. Declarative team had no objections — parity test provided hard evidence. Solution went live, enabling rapid relevance issue classification and near-realtime fixes.

**Result:** Feature shipped on time with business win, not blocked for a year. When declarative system finally ready, migration was smooth — core expr implementation was already consistent, migration only needed store and versioning robustness. The interim solution effectively became early validation for the declarative system.

**Key Thesis on Tech Debt:** Tech debt isn't a "fast vs clean" binary — it's whether you truly understand the system's architectural essence. When you identify the boundary between core and peripheral, you can deliver value quickly while staying aligned with long-term architecture, making future migration incremental rather than rewrite.

### STORY M: Delegation Decision — Hashing for Experiment Platform
**Source:** Experiment platform, seller group testing, researcher collaboration

**Situation:** Team needed to build seller group testing capability for the experiment platform, requiring a stable hashing-based binning solution.

**Task:** Had a working approach (sellerID multiplied by large prime, take high bits for binning) — low latency, stable, reproducible. But the collaborating researcher found this approach unintuitive and preferred standard hash functions with fixed seeds.

**Action:**
Phase 1 — Initial judgment. Believed encryption-grade hashing was overkill for this use case. But observed two things: (1) researcher was genuinely interested and willing to dig deep into the problem, (2) realized "only I find my approach intuitive" is itself a risk — long-term team maintenance requires solutions everyone can understand.

Phase 2 — Active delegation decision. Made a deliberate choice: did NOT push own approach. Instead, handed decision authority to researcher while defining clear acceptance framework — distribution uniformity, performance benchmarks, latency requirements. Role shifted from "solution designer" to "requirements definer and quality gatekeeper."

Phase 3 — Researcher-led discovery. Within this framework, researcher deep-dived and found MurmurHash as a compromise. During validation, discovered that the team's existing self-built dedupe hash had an ItemID distribution non-uniformity issue. Packaged solution as a reusable library, filling a team capability gap.

**Result:** Team got a better result than original approach: more standard, more understandable, thoroughly validated hash implementation. The process also developed researcher's ownership in this domain. The discovery of the existing hash's ItemID distribution issue was a bonus that wouldn't have happened without the deep investigation.

**Key Thesis on Delegation:** Delegation isn't "I can't so you do it" — it's recognizing that even when you have a working solution, letting the right person own the decision produces better results. Value lies in defining what "good" looks like (acceptance criteria), not in personally choosing the hash function.

**Defense Q&A:**
- "What if researcher's approach performed far worse?" — That's why clear acceptance criteria were set upfront. Data-driven rejection, not subjective judgment.
- "How do you decide when to delegate vs decide yourself?" — When the other person has more interest/motivation to dig deep, when long-term maintainability matters more than short-term speed, and when you can clearly define success criteria.

**BQ Questions Covered:**
1. Delegating vs handling yourself
2. Trusting someone else for a key decision
3. Ensuring quality while delegating
4. Empowering a team member
5. Choosing between multiple options

### STORY O: Explaining Technical Concepts to Executives — Allocation Problem Framing
**Source:** Same C2C project, weekly VP meeting

**Situation:** During debugging, discovered two levels of issues: upstream gateway silent overwrite (tactical) and ranking is fundamentally an allocation problem (strategic). Needed to communicate these findings at weekly VP meeting.

**Task:** VP saw several independent tests succeeding and naturally wanted to combo-launch them for maximum effect. Needed to explain why this intuition was wrong, using non-technical language to change VP's decision.

**Action:**
Communication strategy: conclusion first, then expand. Told VP three things: (1) we're currently overestimating the achievable adjustment effect, (2) we may be underestimating impact on default ranking, (3) this isn't an execution problem but a structural one.

Then explained in VP-accessible terms: each policy adjustment performs well in independent testing because it monopolizes the best positions (top slots). But when launched simultaneously, they compete for the same positions — we can't get a free lunch. This is fundamentally an allocation problem, not simple addition.

Based on this framing, recommended VP limit scope to highest-ROI adjustments first, rather than pursuing one-time combo launch.

**Result:** VP accepted the analysis and adjusted project direction. The allocation framing also became the team's mental model for thinking about ranking strategy going forward.

**BQ Questions Covered:**
1. Explaining complex technical concept to an executive (VP+)
2. Explaining complex technical to non-technical audience
3. Persuading others to change direction
4. Influencing through data/evidence
